Control, predictability, and the precision of pain expectations

Notes on Habermann & Büchel (2025, Nature Communications) on the dissociation between control and predictability in pain expectation.

Habermann, M., & Büchel, C. (2025). Controllability changes pain perception by increasing the precision of expectations. Nature Communications.

Read the paper →

Control over a noxious stimulus often reduces reported pain, but the effect is rarely distinguishable from the predictability that typically accompanies it. The two factors are confounded in standard paradigms, which is one source of the inconsistent literature on controllability and pain. Habermann and Büchel (2025) address this confound directly.

Design

Participants received thermal stimuli under three conditions. In the controllable condition, they freely selected among three calibrated intensities (≈30, 50, 70 / 100 VAS) on each trial. In the predictable condition, the upcoming intensity was disclosed but selected for them. In the unpredictable condition, neither the choice nor the upcoming intensity was available. Matching the information content of the controllable and predictable conditions isolates the contribution of control beyond prediction.

Behavioral results

Control did not shift the mean of pain ratings. It changed the slope of the intensity–rating relationship: ratings were less variable and assimilated more strongly toward the expected value. Low-intensity stimuli were rated higher and high-intensity stimuli lower under control relative to unpredictability, as expected from a Bayesian observer with a more precise prior. Model comparison supported this account: precision-change models outperformed mean-shift models on trial-by-trial ratings.

Neuroimaging

Pain-evoked activity in nociceptive regions (anterior insula, mid-cingulate, SI/SII, cerebellum, brainstem) tracked stimulus intensity. Activity in SMA, precuneus, and the periaqueductal grey increased from controllable to unpredictable, consistent with these regions indexing expectation uncertainty rather than pain. The rostral anterior cingulate showed the opposite pattern, increasing with controllability and predictability, in agreement with its proposed role in encoding prior precision and its descending projections to the PAG.

Implications for assimilation effects

The framing treats controllability as a manipulation of prior precision rather than as a separable cognitive variable. Tasks that grant participants control over cues or outcomes should amplify assimilation effects, and the magnitude of placebo and nocebo responses should depend on the agency afforded by the paradigm. This is consistent with the broader Bayesian account of pain control developed by the same group (Habermann, Strube, & Büchel, 2025, TiCS, 29(1)), in which control modulates pain through expectation precision rather than through a dedicated control mechanism. Together, the two papers offer a more parsimonious account than prior information-theoretic and motivational explanations.

Limitations

Two limitations are worth noting. First, several variance results depend on the geometry of the 0–100 rating scale; floor and ceiling effects could compress ratings near the extremes and produce an apparent slope change. The authors address this, but replications should examine it explicitly. Second, the imaging contrasts vary along several dimensions in addition to expectation precision (salience, motor preparation, threat monitoring), so regional inferences should remain tentative. Parametric manipulation of precision in probabilistic cue–outcome paradigms would address both points.

Summary

The behavioral, computational, and neural results converge on a single account. For pain expectation research, the practical implication is that controllability should not be treated as a nuisance variable; it is a manipulation of prior precision.
