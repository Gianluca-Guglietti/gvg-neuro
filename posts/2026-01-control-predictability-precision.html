Control, predictability, and the precision of pain expectations

Notes on Habermann & Büchel (2025, Nature Communications) — and why this paper finally untangles a confound I have been sitting with for years.

Habermann, M., Strube, A., & Büchel, C. (2025). Control increases the precision of pain expectations by reducing the variance of priors. Nature Communications.

Read the paper →

Every once in a while a paper lands that does exactly the experiment I have been wishing someone would do. Habermann & Büchel's new Nature Communications paper is one of those. The core question is deceptively simple: when control over a noxious stimulus seems to reduce pain, is that really an effect of control, or is it sneaking in through predictability? The two are almost always entangled — when I can choose the upcoming stimulus, I also know exactly what is coming. The literature has been mixed on whether control increases or decreases pain, and I think much of that confusion comes from this very confound. This study finally pulls them apart, and the answer it gives is cleaner than I expected.

Untangling the confound

The behavioral design is the part of the paper I keep coming back to. Participants receive thermal stimuli under three conditions: controllable, where they freely choose which of three calibrated intensities (≈30, 50, 70 / 100 VAS) to receive on each trial; predictable, where the intensity is shown ahead of time but selected for them; and unpredictable, where neither the choice nor the upcoming intensity is known. By matching the information available across the controllable and predictable conditions, the authors isolate the additional contribution of control on top of pure prediction. It is the cleanest behavioral dissociation of these two factors I have seen in the pain literature.

Bayesian priors get sharper, not lower

Behaviorally, the result is striking. The intuitive "control reduces unpleasantness" account would predict a downward shift in the mean pain rating in the controllable condition. Instead, what shows up is a change in slope: the relationship between stimulus intensity and rating becomes shallower under control, and the variance of ratings drops. Unpredictable low-intensity stimuli get pulled up; unpredictable high-intensity stimuli get pulled down. This is exactly the signature of a Bayesian observer with sharper priors — the posterior assimilates more strongly toward the expected value as the prior becomes more precise.

The authors then make this explicit with a model comparison: precision-change models outperform mean-shift models in capturing trial-by-trial ratings. I find this convincing precisely because the modeling carries the same intuition as the raw data, rather than being asked to rescue a weaker behavioral effect. The two halves of the paper reinforce each other.

Neuroimaging that lines up with the model

The fMRI results follow the same logic. Pain-evoked activity in the canonical nociceptive network — anterior insula, mid-cingulate, SI/SII, cerebellum, brainstem — tracks pain intensity as expected. What is interesting is the parametric gradient across conditions: activity in SMA, precuneus, and the periaqueductal grey scales from low (controllable) to high (unpredictable), consistent with these regions encoding expectation uncertainty rather than pain per se. The rostral anterior cingulate moves in the opposite direction, increasing with controllability and predictability — a nice convergence with the rACC's role in encoding prior precision and its known descending projections to the PAG.

Why this changes how I think about assimilation effects

This is the part of the paper that genuinely shifted my thinking. I had been treating controllability as something to control for — an annoying confound I would have to model out of expectation-assimilation paradigms. Habermann & Büchel's framing flips that: controllability is a manipulation of prior precision. Whenever a task gives subjects control, it is also covertly amplifying the strength with which their expectations bias perception. That means assimilation effects measured in control-rich paradigms are systematically larger, and the size of placebo and nocebo effects should depend on how much agency participants are given over the cue–outcome contingency. This is consistent with the broader argument the same group lays out in their recent Trends in Cognitive Sciences review on Bayesian conceptions of pain control (Habermann, Strube, & Büchel, 2025, TiCS, 29(1)), where they propose that control modulates pain primarily through expectation precision rather than through a dedicated "control" module. After reading both pieces together, I think the field has a unifying account here that is much more parsimonious than the patchwork of intuitive, information-theoretic, and motivational explanations that had previously been in play.

Mild critiques

Two reservations worth flagging, neither of which I think undermines the main claim. First, some of the variance findings ride on the geometry of the 0–100 rating scale; a floor or ceiling effect could in principle compress ratings near the extremes and mimic a slope change. The authors handle this carefully, but it is worth being explicit about in any replication. Second, the imaging contrasts are between conditions that differ along several dimensions simultaneously (salience, motor preparation, threat monitoring), so reverse-inferring "expectation precision" from a regional effect should be done lightly. Probabilistic cue–outcome paradigms — where precision varies parametrically and control and agency can be partially decoupled — would be a natural next step, and I suspect that is the direction the lab is heading.

Bottom line

This is one of those rare papers where the behavioral design, the computational model, and the neural results all push in the same direction without any single piece doing more work than it should. For anyone running pain expectation paradigms, the practical upshot is that controllability cannot be treated as an inert nuisance variable — it is a prior-precision manipulation in disguise, and once you see it that way, a lot of the older "control reduces pain" literature comes back into focus.
